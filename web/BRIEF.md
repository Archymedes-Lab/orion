# ORION web — design & engineering brief

**1. Information architecture and routes.** Treat ORION as a product with two surfaces and one address per thing. Split the App Router into route groups that each own their chrome: `(marketing)` for `/` and `/business` (top navigation, footer), `(auth)` for `/signin` (split screen, no navigation), `(legal)` for `/terms` and `/privacy` (reading column), and `app/` for the signed-in workspace (rail, no footer). Every decision gets a canonical, shareable, bookmarkable URL — `/app/d/[id]` — and the transient `?q=` hand-off from the landing page is consumed once and replaced, never left in history to re-run on refresh. The workspace routes are `/app` (ask), `/app/d/[id]` (a decision), `/app/history` and `/app/company`. Every route exports its own `metadata` (title template, description), the root ships `not-found`, `error` and `global-error` boundaries, the workspace ships `loading` and `error` boundaries of its own, and the site ships `sitemap.ts`, `robots.ts` (the workspace and the API are disallowed) and `manifest.ts`. No link may point at a route that does not exist; anything internal (design references, labs) is not a public route.

**2. Layout and visual system.** Hold the Swiss Sky system without exception: a 12-column grid with fluid gutters and margins, one type scale (Outfit light for display, Inter Tight for text, Geist Mono for figures), one 4px/8-point spacing scale, one radius scale, and colour only from tokens — night by default, paper for long-form and business, the electric blue reserved for the single next step in a region. Every page opens with the same header pattern (Swiss index label, display title, lead paragraph) and keeps reading text under 62 characters a line. Design and verify at 360, 768, 1024 and 1440 px: no horizontal scroll, no overlapping labels, touch targets of at least 40px. Nothing shifts after first paint: images declare `sizes` and dimensions, fonts swap from metric-matched fallbacks, and asynchronous content reserves its space with skeletons of the same shape.

**3. Interaction and states.** Every screen is designed in five states — loading, empty, partial, error and offline — and each state says in words what is happening and what to do next. Long operations show staged progress ("Reading your question", "Weighing A vs B") in an `aria-live` region and can be cancelled with Escape. The keyboard is first-class: `/` focuses the decision bar from anywhere in the workspace, Enter submits and Shift+Enter breaks a line, arrow keys move through timelines, and every control has a visible focus ring. Destructive actions are reversible: deleting a decision shows an Undo toast before anything is lost. Forms state the rule before the mistake, keep what was typed, and show the server's reason as written. Motion uses one expo-out easing and four durations, explains change rather than decorating it, and switches off under `prefers-reduced-motion`.

**4. Accessibility, performance, privacy and security.** Meet WCAG 2.2 AA: text contrast of 4.5:1 (3:1 for large text and UI), landmarks on every page, one `h1` per route, a skip link, labelled controls, and no information carried by colour alone. Hold Core Web Vitals budgets on a mid-range phone: LCP under 2.5 s (the hero image is the only priority image), CLS under 0.1, INP under 200 ms. Heavy visuals — three.js and the WebGPU shaders — are code-split, mounted only when on screen, paused when hidden, and always sit over a complete static layer, so the page is whole without them. The browser talks only to this origin: the API proxy forwards an allow-list of paths, provider keys never reach the client, third-party telemetry is off, and every response carries security headers (`X-Content-Type-Options`, `Referrer-Policy`, `X-Frame-Options`, `Permissions-Policy`, HSTS in production).

**5. The Aftermath and data on screen.** Every number shown comes from the engine and is labelled as an estimate, not a prediction. The 3D Aftermath must answer three questions at a glance, without hovering: which path comes out ahead, how likely each path is to go well, and when the lead changes hands. It prints those answers as text and percentages in the scene and beside it — a "Comes out ahead" marker on the leading path, the chance of going well at every moment, and a time cursor that reads out every option's score at the chosen moment. It is fully interactive: drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom, double-click to reset, a side view that flattens it into a classic chart, click a path to picture it, scrub or play through time with the keyboard or the mouse. Everything it shows is also available as an accessible table. Acceptance for this brief: `tsc` and `next build` pass, no console errors on any route, screenshots checked at 1440 and 390 px, and the old `?d=` links redirect to their canonical routes.
